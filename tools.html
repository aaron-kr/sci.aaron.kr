About AI

  
    
    
  • Every post needs an "AI-assisted" line — even when the answer is "none."
    • 
    
  • It's OK to use AI for brainstorming ideas or checking grammar. Just say so.
    • 
    
  • It's not OK to let AI write your reflection for you — that's the whole point of the assignment. Submitting AI-written text without saying so is academic dishonesty (cheating).
    • 
    
  • If you're not sure, write it anyway. It will not lower your grade — it's just honest.
    • 
    
  • Formal English phrases you learned for essays or TOEIC ("furthermore," "it is important to note") are not a problem — good, natural English is always welcome.
    • 
  






  
Key words

  

    
Citation
A short reference to a paper — who wrote it, where, and when — so a reader can find it.

    
DOI
"Digital Object Identifier" — a permanent ID number for a paper, like 10.1000/xyz123.

    
BibTeX
A citation format many databases (Google Scholar, Zotero) let you export. Starts with "@".

    
Reflection
Your own thoughts about the paper — not a summary of what it says.

    
Disclose
To say clearly and openly. Example: "I used AI to check my grammar" is a disclosure.

    
et al.
Latin for "and others" — used when a paper has too many authors to list them all.

Example of a finished post

  
This is a made-up example, just to show you the pattern — don't copy the words, write your own.

  

    
K. He, X. Zhang, S. Ren, and J. Sun, "Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition," _Proc. IEEE Conf. Comput. Vis. Pattern Recognit._, pp. 770–778, 2016.

**AI-assisted:** grammar & wording check only

**Summary:** This paper introduces "residual learning," a way to train much deeper neural networks by letting each layer learn a small change instead of a whole new representation.

**Something I didn't know before reading this:** I didn't know that simply stacking more layers onto a normal network can make it perform *worse*, not better — I always assumed deeper meant more accurate. The "shortcut connections" that skip layers were the part that surprised me most.

**A question or note for future research:** The paper doesn't test networks deeper than 152 layers. I'd be curious whether the same trick still helps at 1,000+ layers, or whether some other problem takes over at that point.
